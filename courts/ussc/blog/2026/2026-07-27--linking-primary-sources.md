# Linking Primary Sources

*{{ page.date | date: "%A, %B %-d, %Y" }}*

When you find an error in some data, it's tempting to just fix the error and move on. But when it's an error that people keep tripping over, some explanation is warranted.

For example, take the case of [Aikens v. Wisconsin (No. 3)](/courts/ussc/?term=1904-10&case=3), which U.S. Reports *clearly* says was argued on November 7, 1903.

However, November 7, 1903 was a **Saturday**, and the Court has not done much work on the weekends for at least the last 150 years, except under very unusual circumstances.

You'll notice that *our* data reports a different argument date, and not one date but two: Wednesday, October 21, 1903 and Thursday October 22, 1903.  And why would anyone believe *us* instead of U.S. Reports?  Well, because we include a link to the confirming page in the U.S. Supreme Court Journal. In the top right corner of the case page, click the entry labeled "[Journal Entry for October 21, 1903](/courts/ussc/?term=1904-10&case=3&file=1903.18)", and you'll see that argument in *Aikens* and the companion cases commenced on that day and continued into the following day.

A similar discrepancy exists for the reargument dates, which is why we again provide "[Journal Entry for October 17, 1904](/courts/ussc/?term=1904-10&case=3&file=1904.14)", making it absolutely clear that our dates are correct and U.S. Reports is incorrect.

## The 19th Century Void

As we noted in "[Revisiting The Supreme Court Database](/courts/ussc/?link=/courts/ussc/blog/2026/revisiting-the-scdb)", we rely on the U.S. Supreme Court's "[DATES OF SUPREME COURT DECISIONS AND ARGUMENTS](/courts/ussc/?source=ussc&group=9)" for argument and decision dates of all cases decided from 1791 through 1882.  Unfortunately, the Court's Journals start with October Term 1889, and the previous document doesn't quite cover the entire 1882 Term, so there are 7 terms (1882 through 1888) for which, if we want to verify the dates of a case, we must consult the Court's Engrossed Minutes and/or Engrossed Docket at the National Archives.  And that is exactly what we have done.

Look at [Abendroth v. Van Dolsen (No. 229)](/courts/ussc/?term=1888-10&case=229), which U.S. Reports claims was argued on April 12, 13, 1889.  Except that, once again, we noticed that April 13, 1889 was a **Saturday**, making that date highly suspect.

And sure enough, when you look at the case's "[Minutes for April 12, 1889](/courts/ussc/?term=1888-10&case=229&file=1889-04-12)" and "[Minutes for April 15, 1889](/courts/ussc/?term=1888-10&case=229&file=1889-04-15)", you can clearly see that the case was argued Friday and Monday, not Friday and Saturday.
