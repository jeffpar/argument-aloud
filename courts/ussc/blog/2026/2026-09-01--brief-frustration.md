# Brief Frustration

*{{ page.date | date: "%A, %B %-d, %Y" }}*

Briefs are the cornerstone of every Supreme Court case.  For all the attention that oral arguments get, the briefs provide far more detail.  Oral arguments allow the Justices to ask for clarifications, probe for weaknesses, and convey concerns to their colleagues, but the briefs are invariably the first and last resources the Justices depend upon when deciding cases.

And yet, as you work back through history, those briefs are increasingly hard to find.  The Court's website now provides transcripts of oral arguments going back to the 1968 term, but its archive of online briefs doesn't start until the 2017 term, when it finally introduced an electronic submission system.

The Court explains [Where to Find Briefs](https://www.supremecourt.gov/meritsbriefs/briefsource.aspx) that pre-date their submission system, but the digital archives of LexisNexis, Westlaw, Proquest, or Gale are designed exclusively for businesses with deep pockets and legal expertise.  Briefs become public records once they are submitted to the Court, so where can an ordinary member of the public find copies of these public records?

## Hard Copies

Fortunately, one answer lies in [DEPOSITORIES OF PRINTED SUPREME COURT BRIEFS](https://www.supremecourt.gov/meritsbriefs/briefsource.aspx), where the Court lists eight depository libraries:

- Library of Congress, Washington, DC
- University of Chicago, Chicago, Illinois
- Cornell Law Library Ithaca, New York
- Connecticut State Library, Hartford, Connecticut
- Indiana University Law Library, Bloomington, Indiana
- University of Louisville Law Library, Louisville, Kentucky
- Lillian Goldman Law Library, New Haven, Connecticut
- University of Minnesota Law Center, Minneapolis, Minnesota

Up until 2023, there was also:

- Tarlton Law Library, The University of Texas, Austin, Texas

And up until 2026, there was also:

- Gallagher Law Library, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington

If you dig into the Supreme Court's [Rules](https://www.supremecourt.gov/filingandrules/rules_guidance.aspx), you learn that briefs must be submitted in booklet format (see Rule 33), they must have [colored covers](https://www.supremecourt.gov/casehand/courtspecchart02162010.aspx), they must adhere to various formatting and length rules, and that **forty printed copies** of each brief must be provided.  And a handful of those printed briefs are eventually distributed to the depository libraries listed above.

## Let's Go to the Library

One of those libraries, the [Gallagher Law Library](https://lib.law.uw.edu/home) at the [University of Washington](https://www.washington.edu), is only 5 miles from my house, so let's go check it out.

Oh, wait, I forgot.  The building is "closed to the public".  That *is* puzzling, since the University of Washington is a public institution, but you can't argue with a sign.

![UW School of Law Closed to the Public](/courts/ussc/briefs/uw/misc/2022-10-18-School of Law closed to the public.jpg)

It's doubly strange because, since 2005, I have visited the library many times.  I even audited Prof. Stewart Jay's Con Law I class, Winter 2008, in the same building.  And as recently as [2018](https://web.archive.org/web/20180803225628/http://guides.lib.uw.edu/law/hours), the library was still fully open to the public:

> The Gallagher Law Library is open to members of the University of Washington community, attorneys and other legal researchers, and members of the public.

During the 2020 pandemic, the library closed completely, which was totally understandable, but when it re-opened in Fall 2021, it still remained closed to the public.  Here's what their website said:

> For Fall Quarter 2021, the Law Library facility will be closed to everyone except current UW Law students, faculty and staff. Electronic and telephonic reference services are still open to everyone. Please note that we are an independent library unit and do not have the same access policies as UW Libraries. For updates to Gallagher's access policy, please refer to this page.

While stressing their "independence" and their unique "access policies", there was a distinct lack of rationale or explanation.  So in March 2022, I reached out to the library:

> Subject: Spring 2022 UW Law Library hours
> 
> Your website indicates that you are closed to the public for the winter quarter. Will the law library be open to the public starting with the spring quarter? I have some research to do involving materials at the library that cannot be checked out, and I'm looking forward to starting in the next month or so. Thanks.

This was their response:

> Hi, Jeff --
> 
> We will, unfortunately, be closed to visitors during Spring Quarter as well.
> 
> Try contacting us with your questions. We might be able to refer you to online sources for what you need. We are also able to scan a limited amount of our print materials, within the limits of copyright law and staff resources. See this page for more information, but you can start with us here in reference and we can let you know what your options are.
> 
> You can also check out print material (more books circulate now than before the pandemic). To check out materials you will need to have a UW Borrower's card issued by main UW Libraries (any accounts previously issued through the Gallagher Law Library are now expired). Information about how to obtain a borrower's card is available here. You can request that a book be sent to another library in the UW system.
> 
> I hope this is helpful.
> 
> Sincerely,  
> Alena Wolotira

There was still no explanation as to *why* they were closed *only* to the public, so I decided to take them up on their scanning offer.  On March 21, I submitted a request for copies of the briefs in [Brady v. Maryland](/courts/ussc/?term=1962-10&case=490).  This was their response:

> Mar 21 2022, 05:25pm via System
> 
> Hello Jeff,
> 
> We do not have this brief in our print or microfiche brief collections. Our brief collections are not complete. Our Court Briefs and Oral Arguments research guide can give you more information.
> 
> I'm sorry we can't be more helpful at this time.
> 
> Crystal

The claim that those briefs were not in the collection seemed extremely dubious, and feeling a bit exasperated at this point, I decided to reach out to the (Interim Associate) Dean of the Library:

> From: Jeff Parsons  
> Subject: Access to the U.S. Supreme Court Briefs collection  
> Date: March 21, 2022 at 6:06:10 PM PDT  
> 
> Dear Ms. Wale—
> 
> I regret having to bother you with this matter, but I think I’ve exhausted the normal routes at this point, so it seems appropriate.
> 
> Basically, I’d like to ask for permission to visit the law library in person, so that I can perform my own research of the U.S. Supreme Court Briefs collection in the law library’s possession.
> 
> Today, I tried to use the Document Delivery Service, as suggested on March 7 (see below), but it proved to be a failure.  Now, I will admit it’s entirely possible that there are no briefs from the 1963 case I inquired about (Brady v. Maryland), but I have some experience browsing the compact stacks where all these briefs are stored, and I know that the briefs are not always filed chronologically, and that it’s easy to look under the wrong year.  Odds are, unless someone actually removed the briefs from the library and never returned them, it’s a pretty safe bet that the briefs are there.  Locating them just takes time and patience — and also realizing that, even though it’s a 1963 case, it was briefed and argued in the October 1962 term.
> 
> The UW Law Library is a fantastic resource, and it also happens to be one of the few depositories in the country that holds a fairly complete collection of printed briefs from the U.S. Supreme Court, so you can probably understand my frustration at not being allowed to access these resources in person, especially as COVID finally seems to be receding — but even so, I’m happy to provide proof of vaccination, wear a mask, and whatever else might be required to work within any restrictions that are still in place.
> 
> Please let me know if there’s any way you could accommodate me and permit me to visit the library in person.  I’ve included a copy of my previous correspondence with the library on this matter below (replies seem to come from a different person each time).
> 
> Thanks!  
> Jeff Parsons

She responded, politely, but also mildly condescendingly, while simultaneously completely ignoring my pleas, offers of compliance, etc.

> From: Carla Wale  
> Subject: RE: Access to the U.S. Supreme Court Briefs collection  
> Date: March 23, 2022 at 10:16:57 AM PDT  
> 
> Hello Mr. Parsons,
> 
> I am aware we are one of the few academic law libraries with a fairly complete collection of SCOTUS records and briefs and of the importance of making this collection accessible. At this time, the law school, including the Gallagher Law Library physical space, is closed to the public through the 2021-2022 academic year. We expect to announce future plans for accessing the building in the coming months though.
> 
> I have asked one of the law librarians to double check the collection – print and microfilm – for the briefs you requested. Because the law librarians hold JD and MLIS degrees, they understand the timing of SCOTUS terms and reporting of decisions and how our collection of SCOTUS records and briefs are organized in the law library. They will be able to definitively determine whether we simply do not have the briefs for the case you requested in our collection or they were overlooked in the initial search. They will respond to you directly with their findings, and we will plan next steps based on those findings.
> 
> Your last observation is correct. Research requests go into a system that allows all librarians at Gallagher to read the entire history of the request to avoid redundant work and communication. As such, whichever librarian is working reference at the time a new request or response is received fields the request.
> 
> Sincerely,  
> Dean Wale
>  
> Carla Wale  
> Director, MLIS Law Librarianship Program  
> Associate Teaching Professor, The Information School  
> Interim Associate Dean of Gallagher Law Library & Information Systems  
> Adjunct Associate Teaching Professor, School of Law  
> University of Washington

And yes, the briefs in **Brady v. Maryland** were finally located and scanned, but only after another week of delay.

When I stressed that their collection of printed U.S. Supreme Court briefs is the *only* such collection anywhere on the west coast, and that Federal Depository libraries have a duty to provide public access, I received this puzzling response:

> Sep 30 2022, 12:11pm via System
> 
> Hi, again, Jeff.
> 
> Firstly, I want to acknowledge that the Law Library hears you and understands your frustration with the situation.
> 
> It's not terribly helpful to your overall issue, but the Supreme Court briefs (which I agree, are a tremendously unique and valuable collection) are not actually part of the FDLP. The Supreme Court itself has a brief depository program, of which Gallagher is a part. The terms of our agreement with SCOTUS do not contain the same public access provisions as the FDLP. You're definitely correct that we are the only depository on the west coast.
> 
> Regardless, we hear your message loud and clear. You'd like to be able to access unique resources at the Law Library in-person, and you do not feel that the document delivery service meets your research needs. Associate Dean of Library and Information Systems Carla Wale is CC'ed on this message and is fully apprised of your communications on this subject from the last year. We hope that opening on Friday afternoons will at least be a step toward addressing your research needs, even if this is not entirely sufficient.
> 
> All my best,  
> Alena

I asked to see a copy of this separate public access agreement, but it was never provided. And in any case, unless its provisions differ significantly from those of the Federal Depository Library Program, then not only is its existence "not terribly helpful", mentioning it at all seems completely pointless.

Since these email exchanges, the Library did set up an appointment system where members of the public can request permission to visit the library, one hour at a time, during very limited windows.  In fact, there are entire *months* where no public appointment slots are available.

This is not what the public deserves.

Finally, the fact that the "Gallagher Law Library" is no longer listed on the Supreme Court's website as a "depository of printed supreme court briefs" makes one wonder if the library is no longer interested in maintaining the collection or is exploring ways of further limiting its obligations.
